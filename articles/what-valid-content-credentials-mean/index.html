Photo provenance · C2PA verification

What does a valid Content Credential actually tell you about a photo?

A flooded street appears in a group chat with the caption “downtown this morning.” The photo has a valid Content Credential. Before forwarding it, separate three questions: what did the signer record, does that record match your copy, and is there evidence for the caption?

November 13, 2026 · SIGNA AI Lab

Follow the photo from camera to group chat

Imagine a photographer takes the picture, crops it in an editor and posts the export. The camera file, the export and the copy you download may look nearly identical. Each can give you a different result.

  • Camera file: A camera that never added a Content Credential leaves nothing for a C2PA verifier to read, even if the file came straight from the photographer.
  • Edited export: An editor can sign the output and record a crop or resize. If it received no signed capture history, its record may start with the edit.
  • Shared copy: A service may re-encode the picture, or someone may send a screenshot. Your copy might have no discoverable credential, or its file data might fail to match an earlier record.

Ask for the version relevant to your question. The editor’s export is useful for checking what that editor recorded. For a claim about when or where the picture was taken, seek the camera file and corroboration from the source.

Read the details behind the badge

C2PA calls its signed record a manifest. A verifier such as Adobe Content Authenticity Inspect can show the signer, app or device, recorded actions and source material. Look at the details for the file you actually received.

  1. Who signed, and when? An editor or service may sign a record at export. Treat a signing time as the time of that signature. Look for separate evidence of capture time.
  2. What does the record cover? Read the listed actions and inputs, which some tools call ingredients. A crop may be documented while earlier work is outside the available history.
  3. Does it match this file? The verifier calculates a hash over the file data covered by the binding and compares it with the recorded value. A routine re-export can change that data without a visible change to the picture.

What do Well-Formed, Valid and Trusted mean?

  • Well-Formed: The manifest meets the specification’s requirements for structure and contents.
  • Valid: It also passes validity checks, including signature validation and whether the record stayed intact after signing.
  • Trusted: The signing credential of the valid manifest also meets the verifier’s trust criteria.

Those are manifest states in the specification. To establish that the asset is valid, the verifier also needs a successful content-binding check. A file and its record can pass together while the caption on a repost still gives the wrong date.

Read a real verification report

Open the Content Authenticity Initiative’s public car-photo example. Its report identifies Photoshop as the application that made the record and lists opening, cropping and resizing. The export’s data hash matches and the report says Valid. The input JPEG is listed with unknown provenance. That combination tells you where the documented history starts: you can read what the editor recorded for the export, while the input’s capture history is unavailable in the report.

What to do with the result

  • No credential found: Ask for the file before it went through the sharing service. Some tools can find a possible match elsewhere. Use it as a lead and verify the exact file if you can get it.
  • Signer unrecognized: Check the trust details and compare with the file supplied by the publisher. A signer outside the verifier’s trust criteria is a different finding from a failed signature.
  • File binding fails: Request the file from before the resize or export and run the check on that copy. A mismatch alone gives you no reason for the change.
  • Record and file match: Read the actions and inputs for the claim you care about. If the record says “cropped,” and the question is where the photo was taken, look for the first publication or independent images from the scene.

If you are checking for AI use, look for a recorded generation or editing action. When no credential is found, or the record says nothing about AI, leave that question open.

Keep the conclusion as specific as the evidence: “The signed export matches the file I received. The report does not establish the date in the caption.” Use the public example to get comfortable with an online verifier before deciding how to handle a private image.
