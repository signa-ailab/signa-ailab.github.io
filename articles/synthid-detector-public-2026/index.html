Google's public SynthID Detector: what a watermark check really tells you

One website now checks invisible SynthID marks from several AI providers. A useful advance—but not a universal test of whether a photograph is real.

October 8, 2026 · SIGNA AI Lab

A convincing image lands in a group chat. Someone asks whether it was made with AI. Until recently, checking for an invisible SynthID watermark often meant asking a chatbot or knowing which company made the file. On October 7, 2026, Google opened SynthID Detector to the public worldwide, in English. It can now check supported marks from Google and partners including OpenAI, NVIDIA and Kakao. Apple is listed as a future partner, not a current one.

That makes access easier. It does not make the answer to “Is this picture real?” simple. A detector reports a signal in a particular file; it cannot reconstruct everything that happened before the file reached you.

What changed on October 7?

SynthID Detector is a web service for checking image, video and audio files for supported invisible watermarks. Google introduced a limited version for media professionals in 2025. The company says SynthID has been applied to more than 180 billion images and videos. That figure measures adoption, not the percentage of all AI images a detector can recognize.

There are practical limits. Ars Technica reports that sign-in is required and that the daily allowance is roughly ten checks, subject to change. The service checks files uploaded to an external website, so think carefully before submitting client work or a child's photograph. We have not independently verified the portal's retention or deletion practices.

What a positive result means

If a supported SynthID mark is found, the file contains a signal associated with a participating AI system's generation or editing process. That is useful evidence about a particular signal. It does not identify the person who posted the file, establish whether the depicted event happened or explain every alteration made to the image.

If no mark is found, the conclusion is narrower: no supported SynthID signal was observed in this file. Some generators use different methods or no watermark at all; later editing, cropping or recompression can also affect observability. “Not detected” is not a certificate that an image was made without AI. A partnership announcement does not mean every file from every product of a participating company is marked.

SynthID is not the same thing as C2PA

SynthID is an invisible signal embedded in supported media. C2PA Content Credentials are signed provenance records connected to a file or asset. A visible “AI-generated” label in a social app is a third mechanism. They may complement each other, but they answer different questions. Metadata may be lost on export; pixel-level signals face different transformations. Neither mechanism alone proves that the scene depicted is true.

Start with the question, not the button

If you want to look for a supported SynthID signal, use the new portal and note which exact file you checked. If you want an editing history, inspect available Content Credentials separately. If the image came through a messenger or social platform, review its dimensions, compression and remaining metadata. A screenshot is not the original camera file. Where SIGNA fits

Google's detector searches for supported AI-system marks. SIGNA addresses a different moment: preparing a creator's own photo before sharing. It can add a visible text or logo watermark, or its own invisible SIGNA signal to supported images; later, SIGNA can inspect whether that signal is observable. Supported processing runs on the device. SIGNA does not detect SynthID and does not replace Google's checker.

Public access to specialized verification is a welcome development. Its value depends on knowing exactly what was checked—and what remains unknown about the file in front of you.
