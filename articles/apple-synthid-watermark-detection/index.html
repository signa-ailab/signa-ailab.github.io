INVISIBLE WATERMARKS · IMAGE VERIFICATION

Apple adopts SynthID. Can one detector read every invisible image watermark?

A photo can look exactly the same to you and still carry a signal that software can recognise. But what happens when the person checking it has never used the app that added the signal? Apple’s decision to use Google’s SynthID makes that question increasingly relevant to everyday photography.

November 26, 2026 · SIGNA AI Lab

A camera photograph can have an AI editing mark

Imagine a family photograph with an unwanted object in the foreground. A generative editing tool fills the gap after removing it. The photograph began with a camera capture, even though part of the final scene was reconstructed. Labelling the whole image as synthetic would miss that distinction.

Apple announced its use of hidden SynthID watermarks in June 2026. Its subsequent support documentation identifies selected Apple Intelligence tools from iOS 27.2 and corresponding platform releases, including Clean Up, Extend, Spatial Reframing, Image Playground and Image Wand. This is a change to specified generation and editing workflows, not a claim that every photograph taken on an iPhone carries SynthID. [1][2]

Watermark compatibility is not file compatibility

JPEG readers agree on how to decode a JPEG file. Invisible watermark readers do not necessarily agree on which signal to look for. A watermarking system may encode information by changing small components of an image, or use a learned encoder to produce a signal that a compatible detector can recognise. The decoder may depend on a specific model, signal format, synchronisation procedure or, in some schemes, a key. [3]

Opening the image is therefore the easy part. Detecting a signal requires the relevant technology. A generic noise analysis might describe unusual texture or compression behaviour, but such an observation cannot establish that a particular watermark family is present. A detector must be tested for both missed marks and false alarms.

Three systems, three different jobs

SynthID helps recognise marks added by participating AI generation and editing systems. A positive result relates to the detected signal, not necessarily to a unique editing application: multiple providers can use the same watermarking technology. Apple directs users to a web-based SynthID check that involves uploading an image. [1][3]

TrustMark, developed by Adobe Research, offers an openly available implementation for encoding and decoding an invisible identifier. That makes a compatible independent reader possible. The identifier may also serve as a lookup key for provenance records. Open availability does not make the signal interchangeable with SynthID, nor does a decoded identifier by itself validate the history of the current image. [4]

SIGNA's invisible signal serves a separate purpose. A photographer or creator can add it to a sharing copy and later use SIGNA to inspect that signal. It is not an AI-generation label and is not a universal watermark reader.

Detection, identification and decoding are different tasks

A useful verification tool needs to distinguish between recognising a known signal, identifying its family and recovering information encoded within it. A positive detector output does not automatically provide a readable identifier, let alone a complete chain of edits. Some schemes only need to communicate presence; others carry a payload.

A practical multi-system reader would combine compatible detectors rather than assume that a single statistical anomaly reveals the name of an unknown watermark. It would also need meaningful testing: unmarked photographs, marked examples, previously compressed copies and images with overlapping or naturally repetitive patterns. Both sensitivity and false-positive behaviour matter, especially when a result may influence a person's reputation.

Content Credentials answer a different question

C2PA Content Credentials associate signed claims about origin and editing with an asset. They are not a universal pixel-watermark decoding algorithm. C2PA guidance describes ways to use an embedded identifier or a fingerprint to find a detached manifest, a technique known as soft binding. To make that work, a reader needs the relevant extraction algorithm and, where applicable, access to the repository holding the record. The retrieved information still needs to be checked against the asset. [5]

Consider an image forwarded through a messaging service. Its structured metadata may disappear while a pixel-level signal remains detectable. Alternatively, substantial image transformations may weaken that signal while some file metadata survives. Examining one layer does not replace examining the others.

What a negative result actually tells you

A detector reporting no signal is not the same as a detector reporting that it does not support the relevant technology. Even a compatible detector can miss a mark after resizing, cropping, recompression or other alterations. And an image may have been generated or edited by a tool that never added that particular mark in the first place.

The most defensible workflow is to retain the received file, request the original or pre-sharing copy when possible, use a reader appropriate to the signal you are investigating, and keep its output alongside any signed provenance and other image observations. If a service requires an upload, consider whether the image is suitable to disclose. Never turn an inconclusive result into an assertion that AI was not used.

Where SIGNA fits into a photographer's workflow

Creators need more than an answer to whether an AI system touched a photo. A visible name or logo helps communicate attribution at a glance; an additional invisible signal can offer another way to review a sharing copy later. SIGNA for Android brings visible watermarking, the invisible SIGNA signal and supported on-device image inspection into one workflow. SIGNA now also checks for TrustMark Q and P signals on your phone. Once the detector models are downloaded, these checks run offline without uploading your photo. SynthID and TrustMark are separate technologies. SIGNA Inspect's external watermark check covers TrustMark Q and P.

The larger lesson of Apple's adoption is not that all watermarks are becoming identical. It is that the ability to interpret a mark matters as much as the ability to add one. A useful image inspection begins by asking what was actually examined, which technology was supported and what the resulting observation can reasonably tell us.
